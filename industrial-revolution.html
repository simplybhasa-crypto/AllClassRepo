A turning point in human history · c. 1760-1840

The Industrial Revolution

In a few generations, people moved from making most goods by hand at home to producing them in factories powered by water and steam. The change began in Britain and reshaped work, cities, travel, and everyday life around the world.

Before the factory age

Production moved out of homes and into mills.

Before industrialisation, many families spun thread and wove cloth at home, often by hand. This was called the cottage industry. Water-powered mills already existed, but new machines let owners bring more steps of production together under one roof.

Britain had useful supplies of coal and iron, rivers and ports for transport, and growing markets for manufactured goods. From the late 1700s, textile machinery and improved steam engines helped factories produce more goods, faster. Industrialisation spread to parts of Europe and North America during the 1800s.

The word “revolution” describes a major transformation, but it was not one sudden event. New technology, investment, resources, and changing patterns of work built up over many decades.

Power, speed, scale

Machines made it possible to produce far more.

Spinning jenny: James Hargreaves developed a machine around 1764 that let one worker spin several threads at once. Richard Arkwright's water frame (patented in 1769) used water power to spin stronger thread, helping mills grow.

Steam engine: Thomas Newcomen's engines were used to pump water from mines. James Watt's improvements in the 1760s and 1770s made steam power more efficient; rotary engines could drive machinery even when a factory was not beside a fast-flowing river.

Iron and rail: Coal-powered ironmaking supplied stronger, more affordable metal for machinery and rails. Railways then carried heavy raw materials and finished goods more quickly. The Liverpool and Manchester Railway opened in 1830 as the first successful inter-city passenger railway.

A new rhythm of life

Factory work brought jobs, but often at a heavy cost.

Factories gathered large numbers of workers around machines. The pace was set by the equipment and the factory clock. Many people left rural areas to find paid work in industrial towns, which grew faster than housing, clean water, and sanitation could keep up.

Work could be repetitive, dangerous, and very long. Women and children worked in textile mills, while children also worked in mines. These experiences were not identical for everyone, but many workers had little control over hours or conditions.

Public reports and campaigns helped push for reforms. Britain's Factory Act of 1833 restricted work by young children in textile factories and created factory inspectors. The Mines Act of 1842 prohibited women and young children from working underground. Reform was gradual, and enforcement was uneven.

Industrial growth did not benefit everyone equally. The goods and wealth produced by factories existed alongside dangerous work, overcrowded housing, pollution, and debates about who should share in the gains.

A world remade

The consequences reached far beyond the factory gates.

Mechanised production increased the supply of many goods and helped lower the cost of some products. Railways and canals moved people and materials, while industrial cities became centres of work, trade, and invention. Over time, new jobs and expanding markets changed how people lived and where they lived.

There were serious costs. Burning coal filled city air with smoke, and industrial waste polluted rivers. The growth of the cotton industry was also deeply connected to enslaved labor on plantations that supplied raw cotton. Industrialisation transformed economies while reinforcing exploitation and global inequalities.

Workers organised, protested, and demanded change. Luddite protesters resisted some machinery and the loss of skilled work; unions and reform movements later pressed for safer workplaces, fairer pay, and political rights. Industrialisation did not end in 1840: later waves brought steel, electricity, and new forms of mass production.
